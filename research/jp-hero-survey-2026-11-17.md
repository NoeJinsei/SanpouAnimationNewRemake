# How Japanese logistics companies write their hero headline

A survey run on 2026-11-17, after the owner rejected two place-name drafts: *"None really clicks with me,
maybe because we are emphasizing the names of the location / city / prefecture too much here, when that is
not the main message we want to write on the hero section of our website."* He asked for more examples.

Six readers took a slice each — national carriers, 3PL and warehouse groups, Kanto regional carriers, small
carriers of 20–80 trucks, last-mile and delivery, and companies selling the same four services as his. They
fetched the companies' own top pages and classified what the big line SAYS.

**123 companies, 90 heroes actually readable.** The other 33 are mostly headlines baked into a picture with
no alt text (about 19 of them); the rest are JavaScript-painted pages, servers that refused, broken
encodings, dead domains, and a few sites that genuinely have no headline at all.

The survey's own synthesis says 89 readable, this file's table says 90: one company was counted once
there and is listed here after the overlapping records were merged by url. Nothing else differs, and the
type counts are identical.

**This file records the PATTERN, not their words.** No slogan is transcribed; each line is described in
English. The links are here so anyone can go and look at the real thing.

## The place question, answered with the number

13 of the 89 readable heroes contain a place name of any kind - 15 per cent. That number badly overstates it, and breaking it down IS the answer. Seven of the 13 are search-engine sentences pasted in as a headline ("the transport company in <city>, <company name>"), 29 to 69 characters long, and every one belongs to a small regional firm with a template-built site. Two use the name of the country rather than a city or prefecture, and use it as a measure of reach, not as an address. One has the place inside the company's own name. One puts the region in a small line underneath, never in the big text. One puts a region in front of a list of services. That leaves exactly ONE hero in 89 that is a deliberately written slogan built on places - a 612-staff carrier that stacks twelve prefecture names as the hero itself, because covering twelve prefectures IS its claim. And not one hero in 89, not a single one, is built as "from X to Y". So his instinct is backed, and not as a matter of taste: a place-name headline is simply not a thing this industry writes. Where the place goes instead is consistent across the whole pool - the page title that only search engines read, a small line beside the logo, the sub-line directly under the headline, the service pages, or the footer. The two companies in the pool closest to his own are the strongest evidence: an Iruma carrier of almost identical size and service area, and a Kansai carrier selling the same mix of storage, sorting, processing and delivery. Both keep the place out of the headline entirely, and the Kansai one keeps its whole four-service list in the small grey line underneath.

## Do small firms write differently?

Not by size - by who the line was written for. The small and regional carriers split into two camps, and the split is sharper than any big-versus-small difference. Camp A writes a short, designed line, and the best lines in the whole pool come from this camp, not from the national brands: a 40-truck Osaka carrier wrote a 12-character hero built as three four-character beats on one shared verb; a Kansai carrier with exactly his four-service mix wrote an 11-character question and pushed storage, sorting, processing and delivery into the small line beneath; an Iruma carrier almost identical to his company in size and service area wrote a 12-character line that never names a place. Camp B pastes a search-engine sentence in as the headline. Every one of the pool's 25-to-69-character heroes belongs to camp B, and every camp B hero is a small or mid-size regional firm - no national carrier in the sample does this. So size does not decide the style; it decides the risk. Three differences do genuinely track size. First, small firms lean on the noun ending (体言止め) far more than the nationals do. Second, polite です・ます is almost entirely a small-firm habit: about 15 of the 89, of which only three belong to a national firm. Third, and most useful to him, PROMISE is the largest group among small carriers and the nationals barely touch it - for a brand everyone already knows, saying 'we are reliable' reads as weak and defensive, while for a carrier nobody has heard of it is precisely the thing worth saying. ROLE runs the other way: nine national firms lead with it against four small ones, which is why a ROLE line from his company needs its scale watched.

## What the enlarged sample says about shape

- LINE COUNT. Nearly every hero is one or two lines: of the 89, about 40 are one line and about 33 are two. Three and four lines are rare, and the ones that use them are reading as lists rather than as sentences. The single longest in the pool runs to thirteen lines, and it is the prefecture-stacking one.
- LENGTH. Lines written for a reader cluster at 7 to 16 Japanese characters IN TOTAL, median about 12 to 13. Above roughly 22 characters the pool contains almost nothing that still sounds like a person wrote it; from 25 up to 69 characters it is search copy, without exception. His page allows 12 characters a line safely. That sounds tight but it is the industry's normal length, not a compromise forced by the phone.
- THE COMMONEST SHAPE is two balanced beats: two short phrases of similar length and matching grammar, divided either by one 、 or by the line break, often closing with 。. At least ten companies across four separate readers use it. It is exactly the shape his page constraints allow.
- PUNCTUATION confirms his rules rather than fighting them. 、 is used by about half the sample and almost always in one position - after a short opening phrase, dividing setup from payoff. 。 closes about half, and it closes even seven-character lines, so a full stop is not a sign of a long sentence. Only the list-shaped and search-shaped heroes use more than one 、. Several of the best use no punctuation at all, and those are all the noun-ending kind.
- REGISTER. Plain form and the noun ending (体言止め) dominate. Polite です・ます appears in roughly 15 of 89, and it is concentrated in small regional carriers and in lines that speak directly to the customer. Among the national firms it appears about three times. His ban on です・ます puts him with the nationals and with the best-written small firms, not against practice.
- VERB POSITION, and this is the structural reason his drafts read as wrong. Every hero in the pool that is a sentence puts the verb last and what it acts on first. Not one is built as 'to X, from Y, we carry'. The shape itself is not one this industry uses - independently of whether the first thing in it is a place name.
- NO HERO CARRIES FACTS. No fleet count, no founding year, no number of depots, no list of services - except in the search-written ones, which carry all of them and read worst. Facts live in the sub-line, the page title, or below the fold. His 32 vehicles, three depots and four services belong under the headline, never inside it.
- A SHORT ROMAN OR ENGLISH LINE above or below the Japanese is very common and is always decorative. Its job is to let the Japanese line stay short. The film already carries the roman SANPOU lettering, so he has that tool available.
- SHORTNESS AND QUALITY TRACKED TOGETHER everywhere in the pool. Two readers working different slices independently singled out the same two heroes as the best-written they found; those lines are 7 and 15 characters.

## The three types that fit this company

### ROLE

**Why it fits.** The animation under the headline already tells this story - goods arrive, go onto a truck, cross the region, reach a town. A ROLE line says the same thing in words: this company is part of how an ordinary day works. It needs no place, no customer, no founding year and no service list, so it clears every one of his bans at once, and it leaves the facts to the sub-line where the whole industry puts them. Its natural register is plain form at six to eight characters a beat, which is exactly what a 390 px phone allows. It is the commonest type among the national carriers whose heroes are properly written, and - this matters for his 'it doesn't sound like a person wrote it' complaint - no small-carrier website template produces a ROLE line, so it cannot come out sounding like boilerplate.

**How it goes wrong.** The big companies write their ROLE lines about society, the world, the planet, the era. From a company with 32 trucks and three depots, that scale is a claim the rest of the site cannot back, and it reads as boasting. It also goes abstract very fast: a line about holding up the world gives the reader nothing to picture. The line goes wrong the moment the thing being held up gets bigger than the company. Keep it small, concrete and everyday - the size of a street or a morning, not a nation.

### PROMISE

**Why it fits.** This is what firms of his size actually write: it is the biggest single group in both of the slices that covered small and regional carriers, and it is credible from him in a way it would not be from a national brand, because a customer who has not heard of the company still needs to be told. Its standard construction - two short matched phrases either side of one 、, closed with 。 - is precisely the shape his page permits: one comma in the whole headline, two lines, twelve characters each. It works in plain form, needs no polite ending, names nobody and dates nothing.

**How it goes wrong.** It is the most crowded type in his part of the market and the easiest to write as a cliché. The same handful of words - safe, quick, careful, reliable - turn up again and again across the small-carrier sample, and a line assembled from them IS the problem he has been complaining about for weeks. A promise goes wrong exactly when a competitor could paste it onto their own site without changing a word. The test to apply before accepting one: if any other carrier in Kanto could use this line unaltered, it has failed.

### IDENTITY

**Why it fits.** The commonest type in the whole pooled sample, and the one that produces the shortest lines anywhere in it - the best-written example any reader found is seven characters. 'A bit more basic but still relatively impactful' is a description of this form: a noun phrase with the verb left off, which his page rules explicitly allow. It names no customer, no place and no year, and it fits the phone with room to spare, which none of the longer forms reliably does.

**How it goes wrong.** This is also where the worst lines in the pool live, and the two are only one step apart. One notch below a claim is a label - the category plus the company name - and that is the very SEO headline he has just rejected in its place-name form. Dropping the place does not rescue it, because the fault is the label, not the geography. A bare service label was independently judged the weakest hero in the whole sample. The line goes wrong whenever the noun describes the category the company belongs to instead of making a claim about what kind of carrier it is.

## The traps

- SCOPE - the most dangerous trap here, because it is genuinely true. He really does carry AND store AND process AND run 3PL, which a pure trucking firm does not, so leading with it feels like leading with the differentiator. But every SCOPE hero in the pool that lists the services runs 25 to 44 characters, and those are the lines both readers who met them judged as search copy rather than headlines. Enumerating the services is what makes a hero long, and length is what kills the human sound he keeps asking for. The model to copy is the Kansai carrier with his exact service mix: an eleven-character human line on top, the four services in ordinary prose in the small line beneath.
- PLACE - already rejected by him, and the evidence is unanimous. Of 89 readable heroes, exactly one is a designed slogan built on place names, and that company covers twelve prefectures with 612 staff, so the list of places IS its scale claim. Every other place-name hero in the pool is a search-engine sentence on a template site. For a three-depot carrier it would read as a search result, not as a message.
- CHANGE - attractive because 'the future of logistics' sounds ambitious and modern. But in the pool it belongs to firms with something new to point at: technology platforms, national groups, companies with an innovation programme. From 32 ordinary trucks and an ordinary warehouse it is a claim nothing else on the site can back, and the animation shows nothing futuristic. It is also the single most template-sounding phrase in the entire sample - the exact opposite of 'sounds like a person wrote it'.
- CHALLENGE - the same problem one step further. Striving, taking on the unknown, going beyond the frame: this is national-group register, and from a small carrier it reads as a recruitment poster. That is a live danger for him, because several small carriers in the sample let their hero do double duty for drivers and customers, and the result is a hero that says nothing clearly to either. A customer-facing hero should not be able to be mistaken for hiring copy.
- BENEFIT - well represented (11 of 89) and genuinely appealing, but it collides head-on with his own rules. A benefit line has to say whose problem is being solved, and he has banned naming or narrowing the customer and banned the word for shipper. In the pool, BENEFIT heroes either address a named customer type - one speaks only to flower shops, another only to firms outsourcing small lots - or they slip into polite です・ます, which his page rules forbid. Take the customer out and a BENEFIT line collapses into a vague promise.
- INVITATION - tempting because a question is instantly human, and one of the best lines any reader found is a question. But it needs a sub-line to answer it, and it assumes a reader who already knows what the company does; over a film that has only just started playing, it asks before it has told. The other invitation in the pool is the search-engine kind ('looking for a Tokyo carrier?') with the place name back in it.
- PEOPLE and RELATIONSHIP - three heroes between them out of 89, and both drift straight into what he has ruled out. PEOPLE lines about drivers and staff read as recruitment; RELATIONSHIP lines offer to be someone's partner, which means naming the someone.

---

Sample caveats, as the survey itself stated them: 142 company fetches by six readers, covering 119 different companies once the overlaps are removed. 89 of the 119 had a hero line that could actually be read; 30 did not. Eleven companies were read independently by two or three readers, and they agreed on the message type in nine of the eleven; the two disagreements (西多摩運送, 吉川自動車運送) both turned on which of two texts on the page counts as the hero, not on judgement. One site was discarded for being driver-recruitment copy; no other reader had to discard any. The 30 unreadable ones failed for one reason far more than any other: the headline is baked into a picture with no alt text, which is true of roughly 19 of them. The rest are JavaScript pages that serve nothing, sites that genuinely have no headline at all (a service portal or a prefecture picker instead - itself a finding), three servers that refused the request, two pages in a broken encoding, one expired certificate, two domains that no longer resolve. GAPS, stated honestly: the six slices were chosen, not drawn at random, and they over-cover national carriers, warehouse groups and Kanto regional firms; web search is US-indexed and returns job boards for Japanese small carriers, so the smallest firms (軽貨物) are nearly absent; no reader used a browser, so any hero painted by JavaScript is missing by construction; and where a hero was read from image alt text, the on-screen line breaks are inferred, not observed.
